9-Step Bedside Acid–Base Evaluation Algorithm
  1. Measure electrolytes & ABG simultaneously: Obtain samples within a few minutes of each other to ensure clinical correlation.
  2. Check blood pH validity: Use the Henderson equation ([H⁺] = 24 × pCO₂ / HCO₃⁻) to verify internal consistency.
  3. Identify the primary disorder: Look at pH, HCO₃⁻, and pCO₂ to determine acidemia vs. alkalemia and primary driver.
  4. Calculate the Anion Gap (AG): AG = Na⁺ − (Cl⁻ + HCO₃⁻); correct for low albumin (+2.5 per 1 g/dL albumin below 4.5).
  5. Identify causes of primary disorder: Review HAGMA, NAGMA, Metabolic Alkalosis, or Respiratory differential diagnoses.
  6. Calculate expected compensation (Table 27.7): Determine if secondary respiratory or renal response is appropriate.
  7. Identify mixed acid–base disorders: Uncover hidden disorders if measured values depart from expected compensation.
  8. Use the ΔAG/ΔHCO₃⁻ ratio (Delta Gap):
    • Ratio = 1.0 to 1.6: Pure HAGMA (ratio 1.6 specifically typical for pure Lactic Acidosis due to intracellular non-HCO₃⁻ buffering).
    • Ratio < 1.0 (0 to 1.0): Mixed HAGMA + Normal-AG (hyperchloremic) Acidosis (e.g., diarrhea + shock).
    • Ratio = 0: Pure Normal-AG (hyperchloremic) Acidosis.
    • Ratio > 2.0: Mixed HAGMA + Pre-existing Metabolic Alkalosis.
  9. Determine appropriate target treatment: Address underlying etiology (resuscitation, ventilation, electrolyte repletion).
When to Suspect Complex Mixed Acid–Base Disturbances
  1. Absence of expected compensation or overcompensation: Measured pCO₂ or HCO₃⁻ falls outside the predicted compensation range (Table 27.7).
  2. Normal pH & normal HCO₃⁻, but high Anion Gap: Indicates mixed High-AG Metabolic Acidosis + Metabolic Alkalosis (the classic "normal pH with high AG" textbook trap).
  3. Near-normal pH, but HIGH HCO₃⁻: Indicates mixed Metabolic Alkalosis + Respiratory Acidosis (e.g., COPD + loop diuretics/vomiting).
  4. Near-normal pH, but LOW HCO₃⁻: Indicates mixed Metabolic Acidosis + Respiratory Alkalosis (e.g., Salicylate toxicity, Sepsis, or Renal Failure + Pulmonary Edema).
  5. LOW pH (< 7.40), but NORMAL HCO₃⁻: Indicates mixed Metabolic Acidosis + Respiratory Acidosis (e.g., Cardiac arrest, severe hypoventilation + lactic acidosis).
  6. HIGH pH (> 7.40), but NORMAL HCO₃⁻: Indicates mixed Respiratory Alkalosis + Metabolic Alkalosis (e.g., Hyperventilation + vomiting/diuretics).

Albumin is the principal determinant of the AG (~2.5 mEq per g/dL). Hypoalbuminaemia (present in up to 90% of ICU patients) lowers the AG and can mask an unmeasured anion — hence the corrected AG: AGc = AG + [2.5 × (4.5 − albumin)].
Hyperglycemia Pitfall (Measured vs. Corrected Na⁺):
Always calculate the Anion Gap using MEASURED Na⁺, NOT glucose-corrected Na⁺.
Physiological Basis: Hyperglycemic osmotic fluid shifts dilute Na⁺, Cl⁻, and HCO₃⁻ equally. Using corrected Na⁺ (+1.6 mEq/L per 100 mg/dL glucose above 100) without correcting Cl⁻ and HCO₃⁻ overestimates the AG and falsely implies a high-AG metabolic acidosis.

Urine Anion Gap (UAG) & Urine Osmolal Gap (UOG) Clinical Pearls:
• UAG = uNa⁺ + uK⁺ − uCl⁻: Negative UAG indicates intact NH₄⁺ excretion → GI HCO₃⁻ loss (diarrhea). Positive UAG indicates impaired NH₄⁺ excretion → RTA.
• Urine Osmolal Gap (UOG): Calculated as UOG = Measured Urine Osm − [2(uNa⁺+uK⁺) + uBUN/2.8 + uGlucose/18]. Estimated uNH₄⁺ ≈ UOG / 2. Use UOG when unmeasured urine anions (ketoacids, toluene/hippurate) make UAG falsely positive. UOG < 100 mOsm/kg indicates impaired NH₄⁺ excretion.
Delta gap (gap-gap)

AG excess / HCO₃⁻ deficit = (AG − 12) / (24 − HCO₃⁻)

  • Ratio ≈ 1 — a pure high-AG metabolic acidosis.
  • Ratio < 1 — the HCO₃⁻ fall exceeds the AG rise → co-existing normal-AG (hyperchloremic) acidosis (e.g. saline-diluted DKA).
  • Ratio > 1 — the HCO₃⁻ fall is less than the AG rise → co-existing metabolic alkalosis.
Osmolal gap

Calculated Osm = 2 × Na⁺ + Glucose/18 + BUN/2.8

Normal osmolal gap (measured − calculated) is < 10 mOsm/kg H₂O. An elevated gap in a high-AG acidosis, especially without a lactate or ketone explanation, suggests an occult unmeasured osmole — methanol, ethylene glycol, D-lactate, or pyroglutamic acid.

Low-ECV / low-urine-Cl⁻ ("chloride-sensitive") alkalosis is the usual ICU pattern and responds to saline + KCl; high-urine-Cl⁻ ("chloride-resistant") alkalosis does not — volume-loading is not the fix and the underlying mineralocorticoid excess must be treated.
Saline dosing formula

Cl⁻ deficit (mEq) = 0.2 × wt (kg) × (100 − plasma Cl⁻)  →  Volume of isotonic saline (L) = Cl⁻ deficit ÷ 154

Hydrochloric acid infusion, for severe alkalosis (HCO₃⁻ > 50 mEq/L or pH > 7.55)

H⁺ deficit (mEq) = 0.5 × wt (kg) × (plasma HCO₃⁻ − 24)  →  Volume of 0.1N HCl (L) = H⁺ deficit ÷ 100, via central vein, ≤0.2 mEq/kg/hr

Ventilatory compensation is sluggish: ΔpCO₂ = 0.7 × ΔHCO₃⁻ — hypercapnia (pCO₂ > 46) needs the HCO₃⁻ to rise ~10 mEq/L, and worrisome hypercapnia (> 50) only appears once the alkalosis is severe. Neurologic symptoms (confusion, seizures, carpopedal spasm) are classically taught for alkalosis but are usually a feature of respiratory, not metabolic, alkalosis. Check magnesium before/while replacing potassium — diuretic-induced hypokalemia can resist K⁺ replacement if Mg²⁺ is also depleted.

Serum TCO₂ (Total CO₂) vs. ABG Calculated HCO₃⁻ Discrepancy:
• The HCO₃⁻ reported on an ABG slip is calculated via the Henderson equation.
• The measured serum HCO₃⁻ (Total CO₂ / TCO₂) from a chemistry panel includes HCO₃⁻, dissolved CO₂, and carbonic acid.
• Therefore, measured serum TCO₂ is normally 1–2 mEq/L HIGHER than calculated ABG HCO₃⁻.
• Clinical Pearl: If there is a large mismatch (> 2–3 mEq/L) between serum TCO₂ and ABG HCO₃⁻, repeat the blood gas and chemistry panel simultaneously or within a few minutes to rule out pre-analytical or transcription error.

The Low Cardiac Output / Shock Diagnostic Trap:
In normal health, arteriovenous blood gas differences are minimal. However, in low cardiac output / circulatory shock states (especially patients on mechanical ventilation):
  • Arterial pCO₂ may remain deceptively normal because pulmonary ventilation clears CO₂ from the reduced lung blood flow.
  • Central Venous pCO₂ (PvCO₂) becomes markedly elevated as slow-perfusing tissues accumulate CO₂.
  • P(v-a)CO₂ Gap (PvCO₂ − PaCO₂): Normal is ≤ 6 mmHg. A gap > 6 mmHg signals tissue hypoperfusion / inadequate cardiac output.
Clinical Pearl: In low cardiac output states, an arterial ABG evaluates pulmonary gas exchange (PaO₂, PaCO₂), while a central venous VBG (ScvO₂, PvCO₂) evaluates tissue acid-base status and perfusion adequacy.
